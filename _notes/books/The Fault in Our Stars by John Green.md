### Why I picked it up
Basically,
- I had its movie adaptation in my watchlist for some time now. 
- This book was bestowed with a whole-hearted nod by my sister.

### How the read went

Pretty smooth, actually. Except, well, it hit a little too close to home in places I wasn’t quite expecting.

I went into this book knowing it was a story about two teenagers with cancer. Naturally, I expected the romance, the illness, and the inevitable heartbreak that comes with a story like this. But as it turns out, those weren’t quite what got to me.

It was Hazel. More specifically, the way she thought about her own life ending, and what that would mean for the people she’d leave behind.

I found that considerably harder to read than the romance itself. There’s something deeply unsettling about contemplating your own absence while you’re still around to imagine it. And perhaps even more so when you begin to think about the people who would have to carry on without you.

### What sits beneath the story

I think what got to me most was Hazel’s relationship with the idea of her own death. Not necessarily the act of dying, but the aftermath. The thought that her life might end while her parents’ lives would continue, that they would have to wake up the next morning and somehow learn to exist in a world where she no longer did.

There’s something particularly cruel about that prospect. You can imagine your own life coming to an end, however difficult that might be. But imagining the grief you might leave behind is an entirely different exercise. You begin to picture the people you love going about their lives with an absence that never quite stops being there. The things they’d still do, the places they’d still visit, the ordinary moments that would somehow become reminders of someone who wasn’t coming back.

And what makes it worse is that there’s no real way to prepare for it. You can’t make the loss easier simply by acknowledging that it’s coming. Nor can you reassure yourself that the people you leave behind will eventually be fine, because you can never really know what living with that absence will look like for them.

Perhaps that’s what made Hazel’s perspective so difficult to shake off. It wasn’t just an awareness of her own mortality. It was an awareness of how her mortality might become someone else’s lifelong grief.

We tend to think of death as something that happens to the person who dies. But there’s another side to it: the lives that continue, the people left to make sense of what’s happened, and the memories that become both a comfort and a reminder of what they’ve lost.

And perhaps the most difficult part is that none of this makes loving someone any less worthwhile. If anything, it makes the prospect of losing them more frightening. You don’t get to choose whether someone will eventually have to live without you. You can only hope that the time you spend together is worth the hurt that might follow.

I don’t think a book needs to offer any answers to questions like these. Sometimes, it’s enough for it to make you sit with them for a while.
### Should you read it?

Yes, although perhaps not for the reasons you’d expect.

It’s easy to approach _The Fault in Our Stars_ as a tragic love story, and there’s certainly plenty of that. But what stayed with me wasn’t the romance or even the illness. It was Hazel’s understanding of what her death might mean for the people she loved.

I found myself getting really emotional over something that, until then, I hadn’t quite considered in that way. And I suppose that’s what the book did best for me: it made mortality feel less like an abstract inevitability and more like something deeply personal, bound up with the people we leave behind.

Some books make you cry because something sad happens to their characters. Others make you cry because, somewhere along the way, you begin imagining what their story would mean if it were yours.

This was the latter for me.
